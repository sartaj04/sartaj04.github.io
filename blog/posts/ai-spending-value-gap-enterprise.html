The Gap Between AI Spending and AI Value

Jul 8, 2026 6 min read Sartaj Syed
Enterprise AI Economy Productivity

Two things are true about enterprise AI at the same time right now, and they look like they contradict each other. Adoption is genuinely high: McKinsey's State of AI research puts 72% of organizations using generative AI in at least one business function. At the same time, a huge share of what gets built never sees daylight: Kearney's AI Trends Report puts the share of AI proof-of-concepts scrapped before deployment at roughly 46%. Almost every company is using this technology somewhere, and almost half of what gets prototyped never makes it to production. Both numbers are real, and I don't think they contradict each other at all. I think they're describing the same gap from two different angles.

The Gap Isn't Proof the Technology Doesn't Work

It's tempting to read a near-50% scrap rate as evidence that AI is overhyped and doesn't hold up outside a demo. I don't think that's the right read. In the architecture reviews and system design work I've done, PoCs almost never fail because the model can't answer the question they were built to test. They fail because the question they were built to test, can this model produce a good answer on a curated sample, was never actually a hard question. Frontier models have been able to do that part reliably for a while now. The part that's actually hard, and the part a demo doesn't test at all, is whether the system survives real traffic, messy uncurated data, and the audit and governance requirements that only show up once something is headed toward production.

Spending money to prove a model can answer questions on a clean sample isn't wasted, exactly. It's just spent on proving something that was rarely in serious doubt, instead of on the much harder and much less exciting work of making that capability reliable at scale.

What a PoC Actually Measures Versus What Production Requires

A typical PoC measures answer quality against a small, often hand-picked set of examples. Production asks a completely different set of questions: what happens under load, what happens when the input data is malformed or incomplete, who owns the system when it fails at two in the morning, what the audit trail looks like when someone asks why the system made a particular decision six months later. None of that shows up in a demo, and none of it gets funded when the budget is scoped around "prove the model works" rather than "build the thing that survives being depended on."

That's the real content of the 46% scrap rate. It's not that the underlying capability failed. It's that the project was scoped to answer the easy question and never had a budget or a plan for the hard one.

What Actually Closes the Gap

The organizations that do get AI systems into durable production aren't the ones with access to a better model than everyone else. Frontier model access is close to commoditized at this point, most serious players are working with roughly comparable capability. What differs is the unglamorous infrastructure sitting around the model: real evaluation against labeled ground truth instead of eyeballing outputs, data pipelines that can tolerate messy real-world input instead of the clean sample the PoC was built on, and a genuine operating model with someone who owns the system's failures instead of an orphaned pilot nobody is accountable for once the demo goes well.

None of that is a technology upgrade. It's the same discipline that eventually turns any prototype into a product, applied to a technology that happens to be new enough that a lot of teams are still skipping it.

The Real Conclusion

High adoption and a high scrap rate aren't contradictory statistics about the same technology. They're a fairly precise description of an industry still mostly funding the demo phase, while calling it the production phase, and then being surprised when the demo doesn't hold up under real conditions it was never built to survive. The spending gap closes the same way it always does with new technology: not with a bigger model, but with the boring work of turning a proof that something is possible into a system somebody can actually depend on.
