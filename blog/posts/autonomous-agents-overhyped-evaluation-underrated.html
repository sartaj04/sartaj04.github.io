Autonomous Agents Are Overhyped. Evaluation Is Underrated.

May 14, 2026 6 min read Sartaj Syed
AI Agents Evaluation LLMOps

Two things stand out to me right now about where the attention in AI is going versus where the actual work is. Fully autonomous agents, the pitch that a system can take a goal and run it end to end with no one checking in, get most of the attention. Evaluation and observability, the unglamorous work of measuring whether a system is actually doing what you think it's doing, get almost none. I think that's backwards, and I think both halves of that are really the same observation.

What "Fully Autonomous" Actually Means in Practice

Every multi-step agentic system I've built that actually holds up in production has deliberate checkpoints in it, not full autonomy from one end to the other. A system that parses a prescription or extracts a lab value asynchronously still queues its low-confidence outputs for a person to look at rather than trusting itself blindly. A system that has to decide between a fast default model and a slower reasoning model still routes that decision through explicit logic, it doesn't just let the agent decide unsupervised how much of itself to trust.

That's not a failure to achieve "real" autonomy. It's the actual design pattern that survives contact with production. An agent that runs ten unsupervised steps in sequence multiplies its error rate at every step along the way, since a small mistake early on compounds into a much larger one by the time anyone notices. The systems that work well aren't the ones with the most autonomy. They're the ones with the most carefully chosen boundaries on where that autonomy is allowed to run without a check.

The "fully autonomous agent" pitch sells demos well, because a demo is a clean, curated path where nothing goes wrong by construction. It sells production much less well, because production is exactly the messy, adversarial, edge-case-heavy environment that autonomy without checkpoints handles worst.

Why Evaluation Is the Part Nobody Wants to Fund

The flip side of this is evaluation, and it's underrated for a boring reason: it doesn't demo well either. A good evaluation harness doesn't produce an impressive live moment you can show a room. It produces a dashboard, a regression suite, a set of labeled cases that catch when a change quietly makes things worse. None of that is exciting, and none of it is optional if you actually want to trust a system enough to expand what it's allowed to do on its own.

In the evaluation work I've done, the thing that consistently mattered wasn't a bigger or newer model. It was having an actual ground-truth set to check against and a habit of running the comparison before every meaningful change, not just when something visibly broke. That's slow, unglamorous, and it's exactly the kind of infrastructure that gets skipped when a team is under pressure to ship the demo instead of the system.

These Are the Same Mistake Wearing Two Faces

Skipping evaluation and over-trusting autonomy are the same underlying error: taking a system's output on faith instead of checking it. Full autonomy without evaluation is how a small compounding mistake ships to real users before anyone catches it. Evaluation without a plan to eventually expand scope is how a team stays permanently stuck in a narrow, overly cautious pilot. The teams I think will actually win this next stretch aren't the ones racing to give their agents the most autonomy. They're the ones building the measurement that lets them expand autonomy deliberately, one verified step at a time, instead of all at once because a vendor's demo made it look safe.

What I Expect to Happen Next

I expect the "fully autonomous agent" pitch to get quietly walked back over the next couple of years, not because the underlying models stopped improving, but because enough of these systems will fail publicly and visibly in ways that force a correction back toward bounded autonomy with real evaluation behind it. That's not a pessimistic prediction about the technology. It's the same pattern every previous wave of automation has followed: the unglamorous measurement work always ends up mattering more than the flashy autonomous demo, it just takes a few expensive failures for that to become obvious to everyone, not just the people already building the systems.
